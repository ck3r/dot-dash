    hold for more
    
    
    A key with a mark in its corner holds more: press and hold,
    slide onto one, and let go. A quick tap is just the letter.
    
    
    What it holds are the letter's descendants in the Morse tree:
    the numbers and symbols whose code starts with the letter's.
    V ...- holds
    3 ...--;
    O --- holds 8, 9, 0 and the colon.
    
    
    Everything is on the 123 keys too.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    misheard
    
    
    Morse is two marks and time. A dit is one beat, a dah three;
    the quiet inside a letter is one beat, between letters three,
    between words seven.
    
    
    Hands aren't metronomes. Stretch the gap inside a letter and it
    splits: A .-
    is heard as E . T -.
    Rush the gap between letters and they run together the other way.
    
    
    So ears learn whole letters and words by their rhythm, and lean
    on context; when it still doesn't make sense, ask again (AGN, or ?).
    The ~ shows what the same marks could become, cut differently.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    cut numbers
    
    
    Digits are long: five marks each. In contests and signal reports
    they're often sent cut, the run of dahs trimmed, so 599 goes as
    5NN. The same marks as a letter, read as a number.
    
    
    
    
    4 and 6 aren't cut: their letters would be V and B.

    timing
    
    
    The bar times each press against your speed, from the
    upright line at the left. Key down fills in colour: a full dit
    ends at the dit key's edge, a full dah at the dah key's.
    
    
    Key up fills grey: a letter space ˽ at the dit key's
    edge too, a word space / at the far end. It slows and
    quickens to meet each one on time.
    
    
    Let go within a tenth of a unit of a dit or dah's length, or
    press again within a tenth of a space's, and what you just keyed
    takes a brass edge: on time.
    
    
    The brass lines are where one turns into the next: hold past
    the first and a dit becomes a dah; wait, and a pause becomes a
    letter space, then a word space.
    
    
    Playing back, it shows the timing as it was keyed, with a notch
    where the ideal length ends: a dah held short, a gap rushed.

    suggest
    
    
    Chips beside settings finish what you've started:
    letters the one you're keying could still become, then
    words, then prosigns (with prosigns on). A tap
    keys it.
    
    
    Keying with timing (straight, bug, a paddle), the words last
    until the word space: the pause that ends the word. So the
    faster the spacing, the sooner they go.
    
    
    Farnsworth sets that spacing. Keep the letters fast and bring
    farns. down, and the pause grows: more time to pick a word,
    and more time to think while you learn the rhythm.
    
    
    
    
    Tap keying waits for you, so they stay until you go on.
    
    
    They help, but they work against practice: leave them off to
    learn.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    farnsworth
    
    
    Each letter is sent at full speed (wpm), with longer
    gaps between letters and words, so the message as a whole runs
    slower (farns.).
    
    
    Learn the sound of a whole letter at speed, not dits and dahs
    to count; close the gaps as you get quicker. It's never faster
    than wpm: at the same number, there's no stretch at all.
    
    
    wpm 20, farns. 20  .-   -...
    wpm 20, farns. 10  .-         -...
    
    
    
      
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    privacy
    
    
    Morse is an encoding, not encryption: anyone who knows it,
    or pastes it into a decoder, can read your message.
    
    
    Shared links show the message decoded in their previews.
    
    
    Your history stays on this device until you clear it, here
    or at the top of the history.
